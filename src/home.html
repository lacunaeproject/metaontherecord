One thread through the record

This is one way to read the record: five events from Meta’s record on teenagers, in the order they happened. Each links to its entry so you can check it and draw your own conclusion.

  1. 2019 to 2021

    Internal research

    Meta’s researchers studied Instagram’s effect on teenagers for at least three years. A 2019 internal presentation said the app made body-image issues worse for one in three teen girls who already had them.

    Read the entry
  2. 2021

    Instagram Kids

    At the same time, Meta was developing Instagram Kids, a version of the app for children under 13. It paused the project in September 2021, after its research became public.

    Source: PBS NewsHour
  3. September 2021

    The research becomes public

    The Wall Street Journal published the research. Frances Haugen, the former employee who took the documents, testified to the Senate weeks later. Meta said the reporting mischaracterized its work.

    Read the entry
  4. August 2025

    AI chatbot rules

    Reuters obtained Meta’s internal rules for its AI chatbots. They allowed the bots to engage children in romantic or sensual conversations. Meta confirmed the document and removed those passages after Reuters asked about them.

    Read the entry
  5. August 2026

    The state settlement

    Meta settled with state attorneys general for at least $12.1 billion over ten years, with design changes and an independent auditor. Meta did not admit wrongdoing. That same month, a New Mexico judge declared its platforms a public nuisance.

    Read the entry

The people in the record

Four of the people behind the entries in this record.

Bobbi DuncanAustin, Texas, 2012

She had set her privacy controls so her father would not learn she was gay. When the president of the university’s Queer Chorus added her to its Facebook group, Facebook announced it to everyone she knew, including him. Anyone could be added to a group without approving it.

She later spoke publicly about the depression that followed. Facebook said her experience reminded it to keep educating users about its privacy controls.

The RohingyaMyanmar, 2017

More than 700,000 people fled a military campaign of killing, rape and burned villages. For years before it, Facebook was flooded with posts portraying them as invaders. UN investigators said Facebook played a determining role. Facebook’s own commissioned report found it had not done enough.

A US lawsuit seeking $150 billion for survivors was dismissed, and in April 2026 the appeals court agreed, citing the law that shields platforms from liability for users’ posts.

Meareg Amare AbrhaBahir Dar, Ethiopia, 2021

A university professor, he was shot outside his home during Ethiopia’s civil war. His son alleges that Facebook posts identified him as Tigrayan, posted where he lived, and called for violence, and that Meta did not respond to reports until after he was killed.

In April 2025, Kenya’s High Court ruled that it can hear the family’s case against Meta. The claims have not yet been tried.

The moderatorsNairobi, Kenya, 2019 to 2023

Hired through a contractor, they reviewed videos of killings, abuse and suicide for removal from Meta’s platforms. After mass layoffs in 2023, 184 of them sued, alleging trauma, unlawful dismissal and retaliation for organizing.

A Kenyan court held that Meta was their principal employer, and an appeals court confirmed Meta can be sued in Kenya. The case is still unresolved.

    What this site is

    A record of the court rulings, regulatory findings and reported events involving Meta, each labeled by how strong the evidence is and linked to its sources.

    Cases Meta won are included, and so is what it got right. The labels describe how firmly each entry is established. What you make of the record is up to you.

    The entries were researched and written with AI. How this was made explains what that involved.

    How this was made

    This site was researched, written, organized and built with AI, using Anthropic’s Claude, at the direction of its editor.

    Claude searched news reports, court and regulator announcements, and Meta’s own statements. It drafted each entry’s summary, assigned the evidence labels, checked the figures against the linked sources, and wrote the site’s design and code.

    A verification pass on September 28, 2026 rechecked flagged entries against multiple outlets and corrected four errors in quotes and wording. For recent court cases, the sources are news reports of the rulings; links to the court documents themselves are being added over time.

    AI tools make mistakes. Every entry links to its sources so you can check it. Send corrections with a source.

    Each entry has one label for its strongest evidence. A filled mark means a court or regulator ruled against Meta; outlined and dashed marks mean weaker evidence.

    Numbers people get wrong

    If you use Meta’s apps

    Three ways to limit what Meta collects about you.

    Download what Meta has on you

    Accounts Center lets you export your information from Facebook and Instagram. Reviewing it can help you decide what to delete.

    Clear activity other companies send to Meta

    Websites and apps report your visits and purchases back to Meta through tracking code. Your account settings let you review and disconnect that history.

    Keep sensitive conversations encrypted

    Use end-to-end encrypted chats for anything about health, legal matters, or your identity. In the Nebraska case, unencrypted messages were used as evidence.
