This is one way to read the record: five events from Meta’s record on teenagers, in the order they happened. Each links to its entry so you can check it and draw your own conclusion.

  1. 2019 to 2021

    Internal research

    Meta’s researchers studied Instagram’s effect on teenagers for at least three years. A 2019 internal presentation said the app made body-image issues worse for one in three teen girls who already had them.

How we know

What this site is

A record of the court rulings, regulatory findings and reported events involving Meta, each labeled by how strong the evidence is and linked to its sources.

Cases Meta won are included, and so is what it got right. The labels describe how firmly each entry is established. What you make of the record is up to you.

The entries were researched and written with AI. How this was made explains what that involved.

How this was made

This site was researched, written, organized and built with AI, using Anthropic’s Claude, at the direction of its editor.

Claude searched news reports, court and regulator announcements, and Meta’s own statements. It drafted each entry’s summary, assigned the evidence labels, checked the figures against the linked sources, and wrote the site’s design and code.

A verification pass on September 28, 2026 rechecked flagged entries against multiple outlets and corrected four errors in quotes and wording. For recent court cases, the sources are news reports of the rulings; links to the court documents themselves are being added over time.

AI tools make mistakes. Every entry links to its sources so you can check it. Send corrections with a source.

The evidence labels

Each entry has one label for its strongest evidence.

A filled mark means a court or regulator ruled against Meta; outlined and dashed marks mean weaker evidence.

Numbers people get wrong

“Instagram harms one in three teen girls.”

The internal slide was about girls who already had body-image issues. Among them, 32% said Instagram made those feelings worse.

“87 million people were harvested.”

That is Facebook’s upper estimate, and most were friends of the roughly 300,000 people who took the quiz.

“126 million Americans saw Russian propaganda.”

Facebook said that many may have been served the posts. It could not say how many actually saw them.

“Meta paid $16 billion in scam ad revenue.”

It was an internal projection for 2024 that also counted ads for banned goods. Meta disputes it.

“Facebook caused the Rohingya genocide.”

Myanmar’s military carried out the atrocities. UN investigators and Amnesty found Facebook played a determining or substantial role in spreading hate. US courts dismissed the lawsuit on legal grounds without ruling on those facts.

“Meta handed abortion evidence to police.”

Meta complied with a valid warrant that did not mention abortion. The messages were readable because they were not end-to-end encrypted.

“The FTC proved Meta is a monopoly.”

The FTC lost. The judge ruled it had not shown Meta holds monopoly power today.

Settlement totals vary.

Reports of the 2026 state settlement range from $12.1 billion to about $18 billion depending on what is counted. This site uses the guaranteed minimum.

Small conflicts between sources.

Day-one organ donor sign-ups appear as 13,012 and 13,054 in the same study. Nepal Safety Check figures appear as 7 and 8.5 million. The Flo verdict is dated August 1, 2025; later dates are reporting dates. The September 2026 New Mexico verdict is counted as 43,899,720 violations on the verdict form and 43,899,725 by the state.

If you use Meta’s apps

Three ways to limit what Meta collects about you.

Download what Meta has on you

Accounts Center lets you export your information from Facebook and Instagram. Reviewing it can help you decide what to delete.

Clear activity other companies send to Meta

Websites and apps report your visits and purchases back to Meta through tracking code. Your account settings let you review and disconnect that history.

Keep sensitive conversations encrypted

Use end-to-end encrypted chats for anything about health, legal matters, or your identity. In the Nebraska case, unencrypted messages were used as evidence.
